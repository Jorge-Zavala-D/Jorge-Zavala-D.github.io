<div class="page-intro page-intro--research">
  <p class="eyebrow">Political economy · conflict · development</p>
  <p class="page-intro__lede">
    My research asks how violence and weak institutions shape behavior over time, and whether
    carefully designed public policy can generate recognition, legitimacy, and more inclusive
    development.
  </p>
</div>

<section class="dissertation-panel">
  <p class="section-kicker">Dissertation</p>
  <h2>Breaking Cycles of Violence</h2>
  <p class="dissertation-panel__subtitle">
    Intergenerational Legacies of Conflict and Violence, and the Determinants of Effective
    Policy Pathways for Sustainable Peace and Development
  </p>
  <p>
    The dissertation treats households, communities, and the state as connected sites through
    which violence is remembered, normalized, contested, or repaired. It brings together one
    observational paper on intergenerational transmission, one quasi-experimental paper on
    collective reparations, and one randomized field experiment on transitional-justice
    information.
  </p>
  <p class="quiet">
    Supervisors: Macartan Humphreys, Anselm Hager, and Alexandra Scacco.
  </p>
</section>

  <article class="research-paper">
    <div class="research-paper__index">I</div>
    <div>
      <div class="work-card__meta"><span>Conflict legacies</span><span>ENARES 2024</span></div>
      <h3>Historical Conflict Exposure and the Intergenerational Transmission of Violence</h3>
      <p class="work-card__subtitle">Evidence from ENARES 2024 and Peru's Internal Armed Conflict</p>
      <p>
        Did Peru's internal armed conflict leave traces in present-day victimization, tolerance
        of coercion, and violent socialization? The paper links historical conflict-intensity
        measures with nationally relevant survey data on violence against women, children, and
        adolescents. Its contribution is to connect long-run conflict research with the household
        and school environments through which violence may be reproduced—or rejected—across
        generations.
      </p>
      <dl class="method-line">
        <div><dt>Design</dt><dd>Historical exposure and survey-data analysis</dd></div>
        <div><dt>Setting</dt><dd>Peru</dd></div>
        <div><dt>Status</dt><dd>Research design and data construction</dd></div>
      </dl>
    </div>
  </article>

  <article class="research-paper">
    <div class="research-paper__index">II</div>
    <div>
      <div class="work-card__meta"><span>Reparations</span><span>Fuzzy RD</span></div>
      <h3>Are Collective Reparations Transitional or Transformational?</h3>
      <p class="work-card__subtitle">Migration and Policy Feedback in Post-Conflict Peru</p>
      <p class="byline">With Matthew Bird and Ana María Dumez</p>
      <p>
        Peru's Collective Reparations Program was designed to recognize collective harm and
        support local recovery. Because reparations often arrive as infrastructure or livelihood
        projects, however, they can also expand state presence, market access, and information
        flows. Using the victimization-index cutoff that prioritized communities, the paper
        estimates whether reparations strengthened state–society relations while also reshaping
        community trajectories through out-migration.
      </p>
      <dl class="method-line">
        <div><dt>Design</dt><dd>Fuzzy regression discontinuity</dd></div>
        <div><dt>Setting</dt><dd>Post-conflict Peru</dd></div>
        <div><dt>Status</dt><dd>Working paper; forthcoming 2026</dd></div>
      </dl>
    </div>
  </article>

  <article class="research-paper">
    <div class="research-paper__index">III</div>
    <div>
      <div class="work-card__meta"><span>Recognition</span><span>Cluster RCT</span></div>
      <h3>Recognition-through-Information?</h3>
      <p class="work-card__subtitle">
        Transitional Justice Knowledge, Legitimacy, and Rule-of-Law Orientations in Post-Conflict Peru
      </p>
      <p>
        Can politically neutral information about transitional justice increase knowledge,
        perceived recognition, institutional legitimacy, rule-of-law orientations, and civic
        efficacy? A cluster-randomized saturation pilot in rural Ayacucho and San Martín evaluates
        direct and spillover effects while examining credibility, baseline knowledge, local
        victimization histories, and social diffusion.
      </p>
      <dl class="method-line">
        <div><dt>Design</dt><dd>Cluster-randomized saturation pilot</dd></div>
        <div><dt>Setting</dt><dd>Ayacucho and San Martín, Peru</dd></div>
        <div><dt>Status</dt><dd>Pilot implemented; manuscript in progress</dd></div>
      </dl>
    </div>
  </article>

      <h3>Evaluating CBT Group Interventions in Conflict-Affected Nigerian Communities</h3>
      <p>
        With Nora Chirikure, Macartan Humphreys, Madalena Kresimon, and Alexandra Scacco.
        A two-wave randomized evaluation across 24 communities in Kaduna, Plateau, and Taraba

      <h3>Digital Bridges for Financial Inclusion</h3>
      <p>
        With Angélica Cardozo. A mixed-methods study of how Venezuelan migrant women navigate
        identity, payments, remittances, fraud, and recourse in Colombia's evolving digital public
        infrastructure. Survey evidence, latent class analysis, interviews, focus groups, and a
        regional benchmark identify four distinct user profiles and the institutional constraints
        that keep digital inclusion partial.
      </p>
      <p class="project-facts">Mixed methods · latent class analysis · supported by the Center for Financial Inclusion</p>
