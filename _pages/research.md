<div class="page-intro page-intro--research">
  <p class="eyebrow">Political economy · conflict · development</p>
  <p class="page-intro__lede">
    My research asks how violence and weak institutions shape behavior over time, and whether
    carefully designed public policy can generate recognition, legitimacy, and more inclusive
    development.
  </p>
</div>

<section class="dissertation-panel">
  <p class="section-kicker">Dissertation</p>
  <h2>Breaking Cycles of Violence</h2>
  <p class="dissertation-panel__subtitle">
    Intergenerational Legacies of Conflict and Violence, and the Determinants of Effective
    Policy Pathways for Sustainable Peace and Development
  </p>
  <p>
    The dissertation treats households, communities, and the state as connected sites through
    which violence is remembered, normalized, contested, or repaired. It brings together one
    observational paper on intergenerational transmission, one quasi-experimental paper on
    collective reparations, and one randomized field experiment on transitional-justice
    information.
  </p>
  <p>
    Two sole-authored projects on the political economy of reparations and social-conflict
    data infrastructure are also core parts of my developing PhD dissertation agenda and
    potential book chapters.
  </p>
  <p class="quiet">
    Supervisors: Macartan Humphreys, Anselm Hager, and Alexandra Scacco.
  </p>
</section>

      <div class="work-card__meta"><span>Conflict legacies</span><span>ENARES 2024</span></div>
      <h3>Historical Conflict Exposure and the Intergenerational Transmission of Violence</h3>
      <p class="work-card__subtitle">Evidence from ENARES 2024 and Peru's Internal Armed Conflict</p>
      <p>
        Did Peru's internal armed conflict leave traces in present-day victimization, tolerance
        of coercion, and violent socialization? The paper links historical conflict-intensity
        measures with nationally relevant survey data on violence against women, children, and
        adolescents. Its contribution is to connect long-run conflict research with the household
        and school environments through which violence may be reproduced—or rejected—across
        generations.

  <article class="research-paper">
    <div class="research-paper__index">II</div>
    <div>
      <div class="work-card__meta"><span>Reparations</span><span>Fuzzy RD</span></div>
      <h3>Are Collective Reparations Transitional or Transformational?</h3>
      <p class="work-card__subtitle">Migration and Policy Feedback in Post-Conflict Peru</p>
      <p class="byline">With Matthew Bird and Ana María Dumez</p>
      <p>
        Peru's Collective Reparations Program was designed to recognize collective harm and
        support local recovery. Because reparations often arrive as infrastructure or livelihood
        projects, however, they can also expand state presence, market access, and information
        flows. Using the victimization-index cutoff that prioritized communities, the paper
        estimates whether reparations strengthened state–society relations while also reshaping
        community trajectories through out-migration.
      </p>
      <dl class="method-line">
        <div><dt>Design</dt><dd>Fuzzy regression discontinuity</dd></div>
        <div><dt>Setting</dt><dd>Post-conflict Peru</dd></div>
        <div><dt>Status</dt><dd>Working paper; forthcoming 2026</dd></div>

    <div class="research-paper__index">III</div>
    <div>
      <div class="work-card__meta"><span>Recognition</span><span>Cluster RCT</span></div>
      <h3>Recognition-through-Information?</h3>
      <p class="work-card__subtitle">
        Transitional Justice Knowledge, Legitimacy, and Rule-of-Law Orientations in Post-Conflict Peru
      </p>
      <p>
        Can politically neutral information about transitional justice increase knowledge,
        perceived recognition, institutional legitimacy, rule-of-law orientations, and civic
        efficacy? A cluster-randomized saturation pilot in rural Ayacucho and San Martín evaluates
        direct and spillover effects while examining credibility, baseline knowledge, local
        victimization histories, and social diffusion.
      </p>

  <div class="compact-projects">
    <article id="political-economy-reparations">
      <p class="work-card__meta"><span>Political economy</span><span>Collective reparations</span><span>Municipal elections</span></p>
      <h3>Political Economy of Collective Reparations in Peru</h3>
      <p class="work-card__subtitle">Electoral competition, political alignment, and the allocation of post-conflict reparations</p>
      <p class="byline">Jorge E. Zavala · Sole author</p>
      <p>
        This project studies whether and how electoral competition and relations between local
        and national governments shape the allocation and implementation of collective reparations
        in post-conflict Peru. It reconstructs the administrative and financial histories of Peru's
        Collective Reparations Program and links them to municipal electoral records and historical
        geography. The data construction distinguishes legal authorizations, budget appropriations,
        reported transfers, expenditure, and project implementation. The project examines which
        communities are selected for reparations, when interventions advance, and how implementation
        unfolds. It also develops historical data infrastructure for further research on transitional
        justice, distributive politics, and state capacity.
      </p>
      <p class="project-facts">Work in progress · Potential dissertation book chapter · Historical data construction and research-design development</p>
    </article>

    <article id="peru-social-conflict-database">
      <p class="work-card__meta"><span>Contentious politics</span><span>State–society relations</span><span>Research data infrastructure</span></p>
      <h3>Social Conflict and State Response in Peru: A Relational Database</h3>
      <p class="work-card__subtitle">Database in development · 2026–present</p>
      <p class="byline">Jorge E. Zavala · Sole author</p>
      <p>
        I am developing a longitudinal relational database of social conflicts documented in the
        monthly reports of Peru's Defensoría del Pueblo (Ombudsman's Office). The project is designed
        to connect reported conflict cases over time with information on actors, demands, protest
        and violence, dialogue and mediation, agreements, and state responses. Its construction
        emphasizes documentary provenance, original source wording, and explicit treatment of
        uncertainty. The database is intended to support research on contentious politics, conflict
        trajectories, and state–society relations in Peru. Data construction and validation are
        ongoing; a first public release is planned.
      </p>
      <p class="project-facts">Potential dissertation book chapter</p>
      <a class="text-link" href="https://github.com/Jorge-Zavala-D/peru-conflict-data">Development repository <span aria-hidden="true">→</span></a>

      <p class="work-card__meta"><span>Political behavior</span><span>Germany</span></p>
      <h3>Evaluating Coercive Governments Abroad: Threat, Performance, and Electoral Democracy</h3>
      <p class="work-card__subtitle">Working title · With Daniel Kuhlen and Giovanna Lapresa</p>
      <p>
        This project studies how people in Germany evaluate coercive governments abroad.
        A paired-profile survey experiment varies threat descriptions, security-performance
        information, and electoral institutions to examine the choice of a foreign
        police-learning partner. The analysis distinguishes relative partner preferences
        from approval of coercion and tests a pre-analysis-plan account of threat and
        perceived effectiveness.
      </p>
      <p class="project-facts">Paired-profile survey experiment · Work in progress</p>

      <p>
        With Nora Chirikure, Macartan Humphreys, Madalena Kresimon, and Alexandra Scacco.
        A two-wave randomized evaluation across 24 communities in Kaduna, Plateau, and Taraba
        studies whether group-based cognitive behavioral therapy improves psychological recovery,
